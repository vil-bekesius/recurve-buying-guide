Before you buy

Buy your own bow sooner rather than later, if your budget allows. Do your research first, use your club's experience, and have everything measured and fitted at a shop.

When to buy

There is no fixed waiting time. A good moment is when you shoot every week, or when your club stops lending you a bow. If you can afford it, don't wait longer than you need to:

  • A bow set up for you shoots better. A club bow is set up for whoever used it last, and the next archer after that. Your own bow is fitted to your draw length and draw weight.
  • It's more fun. Shooting your own kit is much more enjoyable than shooting a generic club bow.
  • You'll shoot more often. New kit is exciting, and you'll want to take it to the range.

Do your research first

  1. Ask your club. Coaches and experienced members have bought this kit before and know what lasts. Don't be afraid to ask them.
  2. Know what you want before you go. An archer who arrives with a plan spends less, both on the day and in later upgrades.
  3. Use a checklist. If your club doesn't have one, use my shopping checklist below.

Get fitted at the shop

  1. Book an appointment. Most UK specialist shops fit customers by appointment, especially at weekends.
  2. Ring before you travel to check that the riser and the limbs you want are in stock in your draw weight.
  3. Have the shop measure your draw length, choose your draw weight, and build and spine your arrows.
  4. Try several risers. A riser must feel right in your hand: grip shape matters more than brand.
  5. Allow about three hours. Measuring, building arrows and setting up a new bow takes time.
  6. Shoot the finished bow on the shop range if there is one. Ask the technician to show you the basic set-up: brace height, nocking point and centre shot.

Which grade is right for you?

The prices are rough guides only. What you spend depends on the parts you choose and what you already own.

GradeTypical archerRough complete cost
StarterFinished a beginners course, shooting weekly, first own bow£420–£660
IntermediateClub competitions, working towards Archery GB classifications. You keep your riser and add parts as you need them£245–£300 in upgrades, plus about £250–£650 if you replace a budget riser
AdvancedA look ahead, not a shopping list. By the time you need it, you'll know what you likeroughly £2,000–£3,100 before arrows
Buy once, cry once. An intermediate riser is a perfectly good choice for your first bow, and I recommend it. It costs more on the day, but it lasts a long way into your archery career. Intermediate is the highest grade I recommend buying: as you gain experience, your preferences change, so choose any advanced kit yourself when the time comes.
Where to spend: put your money into parts you don't grow out of: the riser, sight, pressure button and stabilisers. Save on limbs and arrows. They depend on draw weight, so you replace them as you get stronger.

Shopping checklist

Print this page and take it to the shop.

  • Riser (handle), ILF fitting
  • Limbs (ILF)
  • String
  • Spare string, shot in, as a backup
  • Arrow rest
  • Pressure button (plunger)
  • Clicker
  • Sight
  • Long rod stabiliser
  • Arrows, fitted in the shop
  • Spare nocks and points
  • Finger tab
  • Armguard
  • Finger or wrist sling
  • Chest guard (optional)
  • Quiver and belt
  • Bow stringer
  • Bow stand
  • Case (rucksack or hard case)
  • Arrow puller
  • Bracing height gauge (bow square)
  • String wax
  • A good set of Allen keys, metric and imperial: archery parts use both, and the keys supplied with parts are often soft
